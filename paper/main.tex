\begin{abstract}
Large language model (LLM) agents promise to accelerate materials synthesis
planning, but claims of their utility are rarely tested against strong
non-LLM baselines under leakage-controlled protocols. We present SKY, an
LLM agent for inorganic synthesis recipe recommendation built on
composition- and structure-based similarity search over the Materials
Project, together with two benchmarks that evaluate each of its components
against deliberately simple alternatives. In a leave-one-out retrieval
benchmark over 7{,}498 text-mined synthesis recipes, learned composition
embeddings (Magpie) are outperformed by element-overlap baselines for
recipe transferability; a scale-free rank-fusion hybrid narrows but does
not close the gap. In a synthesis planning benchmark on 100 held-out target
materials, the full SKY agent achieves the best precursor-set F1 (0.50 vs.\
0.45 for the strongest retrieval baseline, Wilcoxon $p<0.005$), yet a
zero-cost rule baseline matches all methods on precursor element overlap,
every method predicts the synthesis method family at chance level, and
heating-temperature errors exceed \SI{300}{\celsius} across the board. Our
results support a measured view of agentic synthesis planning: grounding an
LLM in retrieval tools yields a real but modest gain on the hardest
sub-task, while several sub-tasks are not yet solved by any strategy we
tested. We release all code, data splits, and cached model outputs for
reproduction. \todo{finalise numbers after o3/open-model reruns}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Predicting how to synthesise a target inorganic material remains a
bottleneck in computational materials discovery: high-throughput screening
proposes candidates far faster than laboratories can devise recipes for
them~\cite{jain2013materials,szymanski2023alab}. Text-mined synthesis
databases~\cite{kononova2019text} and, more recently, LLM-based
agents~\cite{bran2024chemcrow,boiko2023coscientist} suggest a route to
automated synthesis planning, and a rapidly growing literature applies
LLMs to precursor selection and recipe
generation~\cite{iclr2026benchmark,msp_llm2026,prein2025ranking}.

However, recent systematic evaluations urge caution: retrieval augmentation
helps LLM synthesis prediction, but multi-step agentic workflows can hurt
as often as they help~\cite{iclr2026benchmark}. Positive results are also
frequently reported without leakage-controlled splits or strong non-LLM
baselines, making it difficult to attribute gains to the LLM rather than to
the retrieval corpus it queries.

This paper makes three contributions. First, we describe SKY, a
tool-grounded LLM agent for synthesis recommendation whose retrieval
backbone is similarity search over Materials Project compositions and
structures (Section~\ref{sec:system}). Second, we introduce a two-level
evaluation: a \emph{retrieval benchmark} measuring whether similar
materials have transferable recipes, and a \emph{planning benchmark}
measuring end-to-end recipe prediction against held-out ground truth, with
a corpus-level leakage guard and statistically tested comparisons
(Section~\ref{sec:benchmarks}). Third, we report an honest accounting of
where the agentic approach helps and where it does not
(Section~\ref{sec:results}), including negative results --- chance-level
method-family prediction and large temperature errors --- that align with
independent findings~\cite{iclr2026benchmark} and delineate open problems.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Text-mined synthesis knowledge.} Our recipe corpus derives from
the text-mined solid-state and sol-gel synthesis dataset of Kononova
\emph{et al.}~\cite{kononova2019text}, which underpins most data-driven
synthesis prediction work, including precursor recommendation via
element substitution~\cite{he2023precursor} and ranking-based
planning~\cite{prein2025ranking}. \todo{verify he2023precursor citation}

\paragraph{LLM agents for chemistry and materials.} ChemCrow~\cite{bran2024chemcrow}
and Coscientist~\cite{boiko2023coscientist} established the
tool-augmented-agent pattern for chemistry; A-Lab~\cite{szymanski2023alab}
closed the loop with robotic synthesis. Materials-specific agents now span
literature mining, property prediction, and synthesis
planning~\cite{msp_llm2026}. SKY follows the same architectural pattern but
is deliberately narrow: similarity search plus recipe lookup, which makes
component-wise evaluation tractable. SKY originated at the 2025 LLM
Hackathon for Applications in Materials Science and
Chemistry~\cite{hackathon2025outcomes}.

\paragraph{Benchmarking LLM synthesis prediction.} Closest to our planning
benchmark, a recent study evaluates augmentation strategies for
solid-state synthesis prediction on 674 literature targets and finds
retrieval augmentation the only consistently helpful
strategy~\cite{iclr2026benchmark}. Our benchmark is smaller (100 targets)
but complements theirs in three ways: we stratify method families to test
method prediction at parity, we include non-LLM baselines (rule-based and
retrieval-only) that calibrate the difficulty floor of each metric, and we
evaluate temperature prediction with unit-normalised ground truth.

\section{The SKY system}
\label{sec:system}

SKY couples an LLM (OpenAI Agents SDK tool loop) to four families of tools:
(i)~\emph{similarity search} over precomputed Materials Project embeddings
--- Magpie composition features~\cite{ward2016magpie} and MACE structure
descriptors~\cite{batatia2022mace} --- indexed with exact $k$-NN in
standardised feature space; (ii)~\emph{recipe lookup} against the
text-mined corpus~\cite{kononova2019text}, indexed by reduced formula;
(iii)~\emph{parameter extraction} from recipe text (temperatures, times,
atmospheres, method keywords); and (iv)~a \emph{recursive best-guess
search} that explores neighbours-of-neighbours when the target has no
direct recipe, pruning by retrieval confidence.

Retrieval confidence is $c = \exp(-d/\sigma)$ for scaled distance $d$. We
calibrate $\sigma$ by minimising expected calibration error against
recipe-transfer relevance labels, obtaining $\sigma=0.5$ (ECE 0.17 vs.\
0.41 for the uncalibrated $\sigma=3$; Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}), and
recalibrate the recursive-search thresholds to the new confidence scale by
grid search with a parsimony rule (Section~S\todo{X}).

\section{Benchmarks}
\label{sec:benchmarks}

\subsection{Retrieval benchmark}
Leave-one-out evaluation over the 7{,}498 unique reduced formulas in the
recipe corpus: for each of 400 stratified query materials we retrieve $k$
neighbours and score \emph{synthesis recipe overlap} (SRO@$k$: mean Jaccard
similarity of precursor element sets), \emph{method consistency rate}
(MCR@$k$), and NDCG@$k$ with SRO gains. Baselines: random, element-set
Jaccard, stoichiometric-vector cosine, formula TF--IDF, and a
reciprocal-rank-fusion hybrid of Magpie and stoichiometric retrieval.
Wall-clock latency per query is reported for computational-efficiency
comparison.

\subsection{Planning benchmark}
100 held-out target materials, stratified 50/50 across the two method
families present in the corpus (solid-state, sol-gel), each with parseable
precursor ground truth. \textbf{Leakage guard:} held-out formulas are
removed from every retrieval corpus and recipe store, so no method can see
a held-out recipe; the embedding index itself carries no recipe
information. Methods predict precursors, method family, and maximum
heating temperature; we score precursor element Jaccard, precursor formula
F1 (reduced-formula sets), method accuracy, and temperature MAE (ground
truth converted to \si{\celsius}; $\sim$68\% coverage). Predictions are
requested as structured JSON. All aggregates carry bootstrap 95\% CIs;
method pairs are compared with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.

We compare five methods: a fitted \emph{rule baseline} (per-element most
frequent precursor, majority method, per-family median temperature);
\emph{retrieval-only} predictors that copy the top-1 neighbour's recipe
(element-Jaccard and Magpie variants); a \emph{direct LLM} (single
structured-output call, no tools); and the full \emph{SKY agent}
(gpt-4o-mini tool loop with leakage-aware tools). \todo{add o3 and one
open-weights model}

Table~\ref{tab:retrieval} shows that element-overlap baselines outperform
learned Magpie embeddings for recipe transferability (SRO@5 0.53 vs.\
0.33): materials sharing precursor chemistry are found more reliably by
element sets than by dense composition features, which distribute
attention across property-relevant but synthesis-irrelevant dimensions.
The rank-fusion hybrid narrows the gap (SRO@5 0.41) but element-set
retrieval remains the strongest backbone, and we adopt it for the
planning benchmark's retrieval predictor accordingly. Structure-mode
(MACE) retrieval is evaluated in the ablation (Section~S\todo{X});
unlike element-set baselines it extends to CIF inputs.

Three findings stand out in Table~\ref{tab:planning}. First, \textbf{the
SKY agent achieves the best precursor formula F1} (0.497 [0.44, 0.55]),
significantly above the strongest retrieval baseline (0.448; Wilcoxon
$p=0.005$, Cohen's $d=0.29$): retrieving several neighbours and letting
the LLM adapt their recipes beats copying any single neighbour, and beats
the ungrounded LLM (0.433). Second, \textbf{cheap baselines set a high
floor on lenient metrics}: on precursor \emph{element} overlap the rule
baseline (0.821) is indistinguishable from the agent (0.813) --- element
identity is nearly determined by the target composition, so only the
compound-level F1 discriminates methods. Third, \textbf{two sub-tasks are
unsolved by all methods}: method-family accuracy is at chance
($\approx$0.5 on the balanced set) for every method including the agent,
and temperature MAE is $\ge$\SI{310}{\celsius} throughout. Magpie-based
retrieval fails outright on 42/100 targets, echoing its Table~1 weakness.

Our modest agent-over-LLM gain, alongside chance-level method prediction,
is consistent with the finding of ref.~\citenum{iclr2026benchmark} that
retrieval is the reliably useful augmentation while added agentic
machinery often contributes little.

\section{Discussion and limitations}

\paragraph{What the agent buys.} The agent's advantage concentrates
exactly where reasoning over retrieved evidence can help: choosing
\emph{which} compounds to adapt from \emph{which} neighbours. Where the
answer is nearly determined by composition (element overlap) or barely
inferable from composition alone (method family, temperature), the agent
neither helps nor hurts.

\paragraph{Limitations.} (i)~The corpus covers only solid-state and
sol-gel routes, inherited from the text-mining pipeline's biases
\cite{kononova2019text}; (ii)~results are for a single closed model
(gpt-4o-mini) at $n=100$ \todo{address with o3 + open-model reruns and
$n=400$}; (iii)~formula-level F1 penalises hydrate/anhydrous mismatches
identically for all methods but understates chemical validity; (iv)~no
experimental validation is attempted --- predictions are judged against
literature ground truth only; (v)~structure-mode retrieval requires the
MACE embedding index, which we \todo{regenerate and evaluate}.

\section{Conclusions}

Tool-grounded LLM agents deliver a real but narrow improvement in
inorganic synthesis planning: better precursor selection, no better method
or temperature prediction. Rigorous baselines and leakage control shrink
--- but do not eliminate --- the apparent gains of agentic approaches. We
argue such evaluation-first reporting is a prerequisite for trustworthy
progress in LLM-driven synthesis planning, and release the full framework
to support it.
